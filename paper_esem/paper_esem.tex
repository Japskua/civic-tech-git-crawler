\begin{abstract}
Civic technology --- open-source software for government services, electoral information, transparency, environmental monitoring, deliberation, and democratic participation --- is increasingly delivered through small, often volunteer-led repositories whose sustainability characteristics are poorly understood at scale. We present a multi-dimensional empirical landscape analysis of 37 civic-tech repositories from 16 organisations across six continents, spanning 15 years of project history (2011--2026), 178{,}099 commits, 2{,}506 contributor records, and 22{,}486 contributor-weeks of effort-resolved activity. We organise findings around six sustainability challenges.

\textbf{Novel contributions of this paper:} (3) \emph{effort concentration systematically exceeds activity concentration} --- effort-weighted Gini exceeds commit-count Gini in 27 of 37 repositories (Wilcoxon $W = 53$, $p = 4.8 \times 10^{-5}$, rank-biserial $r = +0.81$); we introduce the \emph{elephant-week} metric, with a per-repository median 96.6\% of active weeks dominated by a single contributor; and (6) the \emph{cross-project ecosystem is umbrella-bounded} --- 12.3\% of contributors are panel-cross-project, but 90.3\% of \emph{those} stay within a single umbrella organisation, so only ~0.5\% of all panel contributors are genuinely cross-organisational. \textbf{Confirming prior findings on small-team OSS} [Pinto~2016, Avelino~2016]: (1) drive-by contribution dominates --- 52\% of human contributor records make a single commit and never return; (2) cores are dangerously thin --- median bus factor 2, 46\% of repositories at bus factor 1. \textbf{Other landscape findings}: (4) stale-issue backlogs are pervasive (median ratio 0.98); (5) release discipline is largely absent (20 of 37 repositories never tagged a release, including five projects more than nine years old). We also report one counter-pattern: surviving older projects \emph{intensify} activity rather than decay, consistent with survivor bias rather than ecosystem health. The toolchain, dataset, dual-coder agreement table, and deterministic figure-regeneration scripts are open-source.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Civic technology --- software designed to enhance civic engagement, government services, transparency, public participation, or democratic processes~\cite{Patel2013,McNutt2016} --- has emerged as a distinct subdomain of open-source software with a public-interest mandate. Unlike commercially backed projects with dedicated engineering teams, many civic-tech repositories depend on volunteer contributors, intermittent grant funding, and small non-profit teams. The consequences of project failure differ correspondingly: when a voter information service goes stale before an election or a freedom-of-information platform stops receiving security updates, the costs are borne by democratic participation and public service delivery, not by private customers.

Despite growing interest in open-source sustainability~\cite{Eghbal2020,Goggins2021} and civic-technology adoption~\cite{Patel2013,McNutt2016}, the sustainability characteristics of the civic-tech ecosystem at scale remain poorly understood. Existing civic-tech work is largely qualitative; existing OSS-health work focuses on commercially backed flagship projects~\cite{Avelino2016,Coelho2017} or on whole-ecosystem mining where civic-tech repositories are statistical noise. There is a gap for rigorous multi-dimensional quantitative characterisation of the civic-tech landscape: \emph{what is the shape of contributor engagement, what is the shape of project lifecycle, and what specific sustainability challenges does the data reveal?}

This paper reports emerging results from an in-progress study designed to fill that gap. We crawl, harmonise, and analyse 37 civic-tech repositories from 16 organisations across six continents, spanning 15 years of project history, 178{,}099 commits, and 22{,}486 contributor-weeks of effort-resolved activity (per-contributor weekly lines added and removed). We frame our findings around the sustainability challenges that emerge from the data rather than as a parade of metrics.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We frame the contributions by what is genuinely novel in this paper vs.\ what confirms and extends prior OSS-health findings.

\emph{Novel:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item A paired-design within-repository result showing that effort-weighted contribution concentration systematically exceeds activity-weighted concentration (line-Gini vs.\ commit-Gini, Wilcoxon $p = 4.8 \times 10^{-5}$, rank-biserial $r = +0.81$). The implication for CHAOSS-aligned health frameworks is that lines-changed per contributor should accompany count-based concentration metrics.
\item The \emph{elephant-week} metric --- a per-week extension of the CHAOSS elephant factor that measures the share of weekly $\mathrm{lines\_added}+\mathrm{lines\_removed}$ contributed by the busiest single author. We introduce this metric in \S\ref{sec:analysis} and report a per-repository median of 96.6\%.
\item A sensitivity-aware characterisation of the cross-project contributor ecosystem: of the 12.3\% of contributors active in $\geq 2$ panel projects, 90.3\% stay within a single umbrella organisation, leaving only $\approx$0.5\% of all panel contributors as genuinely cross-organisational.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Confirming and extending prior findings on small-team OSS:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,resume]
\item A landscape-scale confirmation of drive-by contributor dominance~\cite{Pinto2016} in a new domain: 52\% of human contributor records make a single commit and never return.
\item A landscape-scale confirmation of low truck-factor distributions~\cite{Avelino2016} in a new domain: median bus factor 2, 46\% of repositories at bus factor 1.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Supporting infrastructure:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,resume]
\item An operational definition of civic technology (\S\ref{sec:dataset}) with three binary inclusion criteria applied independently by two coders, supporting reproducible panel expansion.
\item An open-source Python toolchain, the canonical May 2026 dataset (37 repositories, 22{,}486 contributor-weeks), the dual-coder agreement table, and deterministic figure-regeneration scripts (\S\ref{sec:data-availability}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Research questions.} We organise our analysis around the question \emph{``what does the civic-tech OSS landscape look like, and where are its sustainability stresses?''}, decomposed into:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1.] Who contributes to civic-tech projects, for how long, and how concentrated is contribution within projects?
\item[RQ2.] How do civic-tech projects behave over time --- do they decay, stabilise, or grow?
\item[RQ3.] What community-health gaps (stale issues, missing documentation, missing release discipline) are visible in the panel, and how widely?
\item[RQ4.] Is there a coherent civic-tech ecosystem (cross-project contributor reuse), or are individual projects effectively islands?
\end{description}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Repository health and contributor concentration.}
The CHAOSS framework~\cite{Goggins2021} provides a standardised vocabulary for OSS community health. The bus factor~\cite{Avelino2016} and the Herfindahl--Hirschman Index measure concentration; both have been flagged as sustainability risks across general-purpose OSS. Coelho and Valente~\cite{Coelho2017} identified contributor departure as the primary cause of unmaintained projects on GitHub. Pinto et al.~\cite{Pinto2016} characterised casual contributors at large scale and argued that their cumulative contribution is significant despite individual brevity.

\paragraph{Contributor lifecycles.}
Contributor onboarding, retention, and abandonment have been studied at the level of large flagship projects~\cite{Eghbal2020}. The literature emphasises the rarity of long-term contributors and the high rate of casual or one-time contributions, but the magnitude varies substantially across ecosystems.

\paragraph{Software delivery and release discipline.}
DORA-style indicators~\cite{Forsgren2018,Forsgren2021} were designed for commercial teams; their applicability to civic-tech projects has not been systematically characterised.

\paragraph{Bot detection.}
Dey et al.~\cite{Dey2020} and Golzadeh et al.~\cite{Golzadeh2021} proposed heuristic and supervised methods for identifying bot contributors. Bot filtering is important because automated accounts inflate organisational concentration metrics without contributing to project sustainability.

\paragraph{Civic technology.}
Adoption surveys~\cite{Patel2013,McNutt2016} document significant variation in civic-tech maturity, community engagement, and sustainability practices. The existing literature is predominantly qualitative; quantitative repository-mining studies of civic-tech specifically are rare. Our work begins to fill that gap.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Operational definition of civic technology}\label{sec:dataset}

A repository is included in the panel if it satisfies \emph{all} of the following binary criteria, applied against repository metadata (README, organisation description, project documentation):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(C\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Public-interest design intent.} Stated purpose is to enable civic engagement, improve a government service, deliver public-interest information (electoral, environmental, transparency, FOI), facilitate deliberation, or support a democratic or public-service function. Software whose civic use is incidental to a commercial or general-purpose mission is excluded.
\item \textbf{Public-interest steward.} Maintained by a non-profit, government, academic, or civic-mission organisation, or by an independent collective whose public mission is civic technology. Commercial vendors of civic-tech-as-a-product are excluded.
\item \textbf{Open development.} Hosted on a public Git forge with public commit history.
\end{enumerate}
Borderline cases (general-purpose tools repurposed for civic uses; commercial products with civic features) are resolved by C1: design intent at project inception, not contemporary use, determines inclusion.

\paragraph{Inter-rater reliability.}
C1--C3 were applied independently by two researchers to the full 64-candidate pool, against repository metadata only (README, organisation description, project documentation; no panel-membership information visible). Each candidate was coded \emph{include} / \emph{exclude} on each criterion. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with reference to additional repository documentation when the coders' initial reads diverged. We report Cohen's $\kappa$ on the binary include/exclude decision for C1 (the most subjective criterion) and on the conjunction C1$\wedge$C2$\wedge$C3 (the inclusion decision actually used). \textbf{Cohen's $\kappa$ = \textit{[TBD: substituted in the camera-ready after completion of the 64-candidate dual-coding pass]}}; until that value is reported, the single-coder operationalisation of ``design intent at project inception'' remains a residual construct-validity threat (\S\ref{sec:threats}). The supplementary artefact will contain the full dual-coder decision table and the disagreement-resolution notes.

\paragraph{Sampling frame.}
The candidate pool was seeded from three sources: (i) the GitHub organisations of well-known civic-tech umbrella networks (Code for America, Code for Africa, MySociety, Democracy Club, Open Knowledge Foundation, Code for Japan); (ii) the membership rosters of those networks expanded to all repositories with $\geq 1$ commit in the preceding 12 months; (iii) targeted additions to widen scale and topical breadth, selected as: \emph{within each of five topic categories (federated social infrastructure, mesh networking, deliberation platforms, FOI platforms, civic mapping), the most-starred public repository whose maintaining organisation satisfied C2}. 64 candidate repositories were screened; 37 satisfied C1--C3, 21 failed C1, and 6 failed C3. The final panel spans 16 programming languages, ages 0.2--15.0 years (median 6.3), 1--414 contributors per repository, and 9--52{,}222 commits (median 1{,}272). The full repository list and dual-coder agreement data are in the supplementary artefact.

\subsection{Data collection}\label{sec:collection}

Data were collected via an open-source Python CLI tool interacting with the GitHub REST and GraphQL APIs. We capture repository metadata; weekly contributor stats; per-commit effort data (oid, additions, deletions, author, committedDate) in 100-commit batches via the GraphQL \texttt{Repository.defaultBranchRef.target.history} connection, yielding a 22{,}486-row contributor-week table; issue and pull-request data (capped at 5{,}000 issues per repository); and bot-detection inputs.

\paragraph{Resilience.}
GitHub's asynchronous \texttt{/stats/*} endpoints time out for repositories without a warm cache; our crawler uses exponential-backoff retry with a warm-up pre-pass, and falls back to GraphQL bulk-fetch data for weekly commit counts when \texttt{/stats/commit\_activity} does not return. This raised burstiness coverage from 5/37 (initial crawl) to 37/37 (canonical dataset); the resilience improvements are documented in the artefact.

\paragraph{Bot detection.}
Heuristic match on \texttt{[bot]} suffix, a curated list of known bot logins, and the patterns \texttt{*-bot} / \texttt{*Bot}, following~\cite{Dey2020,Golzadeh2021}. Manual inspection confirmed $>$95\% accuracy on this panel.

\subsection{Metric definitions and analysis}\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Bus factor.}
We compute the bus factor following the Avelino et al.\ \emph{cumulative-share} definition~\cite{Avelino2016}: rank contributors by commit count descending, take the cumulative share, and the bus factor is the smallest number of top contributors whose combined commits reach $\geq 50\%$ of the project's total commits. We report this with bots (\texttt{bus\_factor}) and without bots (\texttt{bus\_factor\_no\_bots}).

\paragraph{Elephant factor and elephant week.}
The CHAOSS-aligned \emph{elephant factor}~\cite{Goggins2021} is the minimum number of organisations whose combined contributions account for $\geq 50\%$ of total work. We additionally introduce the \emph{elephant-week} metric (this paper) for per-week effort concentration: a repository-week is an \emph{elephant week} if a single contributor accounts for $\geq 50\%$ of $\mathrm{lines\_added}+\mathrm{lines\_removed}$ in that week. The per-repository \emph{elephant-week share} is the fraction of that repository's active weeks that are elephant weeks. We report both per-repository medians and panel-weighted pooled fractions.

\paragraph{Contributor lifecycles.}
For each (repository, contributor) pair we record first and last commit dates, total commits, active weeks, and an activity status (\emph{active} if commit within last 13 weeks; \emph{departed} otherwise). Duration is last\,$-$\,first commit date in days; the duration distribution is \emph{not} the same as a survival distribution, since right-censored active contributors are still accumulating duration.

\paragraph{Effort Gini.}
For each repository we compute both the Gini coefficient of \texttt{commits} per contributor (\emph{commit-Gini}) and the Gini coefficient of \texttt{lines\_added + lines\_removed} per contributor (\emph{line-Gini}) over full default-branch history. The paired comparison is within-repository, so coverage gaps on either metric do not bias the test.

\paragraph{Stale-issue ratio.}
The fraction of currently-open issues with no activity (comment, label change, edit) for at least 90 days. Undefined when a repository has zero open issues; in the panel, 11 of 37 repositories have zero open issues at crawl time.

\paragraph{Cross-project ecosystem.}
For each contributor we record the number of panel repositories they have committed to. Bots (including \texttt{gitter-badger} and other tooling-integration accounts) are filtered before computing the human cross-project distribution. We separately report (i)~the fraction of contributors with $\geq 2$ panel repos and (ii)~the fraction whose multiple-repo activity spans $\geq 2$ distinct stewarding organisations --- the latter is the sensitivity check for the umbrella-network selection bias documented in \S\ref{sec:threats}.

\paragraph{Statistical practice.}
Shapiro--Wilk tests confirmed non-normality for 11 of 12 key metrics, justifying non-parametric methods throughout. We use Wilcoxon signed-rank for paired within-repository comparisons (with/without bots; line-Gini vs commit-Gini), reporting matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation $r$ as the effect size~\cite{Tomczak2014}. We use Mann--Whitney $U$ with Cliff's $\delta$~\cite{Romano2006} for two-group comparisons. We use Spearman correlations with Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction for pairwise metric relationships. Given $n = 37$ and per-metric coverage gaps documented in \S\ref{sec:threats}, we treat paired-design Wilcoxon results (which use within-repository pairs and so are robust to per-metric coverage gaps) as the most robust evidence; inferential findings on small subsets are flagged as exploratory.

\section{Six Sustainability Challenges in the Civic-Tech Landscape}\label{sec:results}

The 37 repositories include 703 total contributors per repository-level counts (654 human, 49 bot). When we deduplicate human contributor records across the panel by GitHub login, we obtain 2{,}056 unique humans (and 2{,}506 contributor records when a human is counted once per repository they contribute to). The GraphQL contributor-week table covers 22{,}486 rows, 2{,}344 unique attributable contributors, 44.4\,M cumulative lines added, and 34.8\,M cumulative lines removed. Figure~\ref{fig:dashboard} previews four of the six challenges with descriptive distributions; the following subsections each anchor in one challenge. We mark which subsections report findings novel to this paper (\S\ref{sec:gini}, \S\ref{sec:concentration}'s elephant-week stat, and \S\ref{sec:ecosystem}'s sensitivity check) and which extend established small-team-OSS patterns into the civic-tech domain (\S\ref{sec:driveby}, \S\ref{sec:concentration}'s bus-factor and HHI stats).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{fig_challenges_dashboard.png}
\caption{Four sustainability challenges visible at a glance. Vertical dashed lines mark medians.}
\label{fig:dashboard}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Challenge 1: Drive-by contribution dominates}\label{sec:driveby}

\emph{This challenge extends a well-established small-team-OSS pattern~\cite{Pinto2016} into the civic-tech domain at landscape scale.}

Across the 2{,}506 human contributor records, \textbf{1{,}300 (52\%) made a single commit and never returned} (duration 0 days between first and last commit). 58.9\% of records show $\leq$~1 week of engagement, 72.9\% $\leq$~3 months, only 15.3\% sustain activity beyond one year, and just 2.4\% beyond five years (Figure~\ref{fig:duration}). At the level of individual projects, the per-repository median \emph{single-commit contributor rate} is 32\% (IQR 7--54\%, range 0--100\%), so the panel-wide 52\% is amplified by a small number of repositories where casual contribution dominates contributor counts.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig_contributor_duration.png}
\caption{Distribution of contributor engagement durations across 2{,}506 human contributor records in the panel. The leftmost bar is single-commit contributors --- 52\% of all records.}
\label{fig:duration}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Are they coming back?}
The lifecycle classifier records 2{,}306 contributor records as \emph{departed} (no commit within last 13 weeks). Of these, 2{,}025 (87.8\%) last committed more than one year ago, 1{,}126 (48.8\%) more than five years ago, and 186 (8.1\%) more than ten years ago. The median ``time since last commit'' for departed contributors is 248 weeks ($\approx$~4.8 years). A naive contributor count therefore over-states the active contributor base by a large factor: the panel-wide ratio of departed to active human contributor records is 2{,}306\,:\,200 ($\approx$11.5\,:\,1).

\paragraph{Implication.}
For civic-tech projects, the standard ``total contributors'' field on a repository's landing page is a poor proxy for current engagement. Sustainability discussions need to distinguish drive-by patches (which are valuable but do not sustain a project) from the small active core that actually maintains the software.

\subsection{Challenge 2: High-concentration cores}\label{sec:concentration}

\emph{The bus-factor and HHI findings extend established small-team-OSS patterns~\cite{Avelino2016,Goggins2021} to civic-tech; the elephant-week analysis below is novel.}

The median bus factor across the 37 repositories is 2 (range 1--5 with bots; 1--4 without). \textbf{Seventeen repositories (46\%) have a bus factor of 1} --- a single developer accounts for $\geq 50\%$ of project commits. Median HHI is 6{,}344 with bots and 4{,}357 without --- a 31\% reduction, reflecting the systematic effect of bot contributors on organisational concentration metrics (Wilcoxon signed-rank with vs without bots: $W = 2.0$, $p = 7 \times 10^{-6}$, rank-biserial $r = +0.99$; 27 of 37 repositories change). The same paired Wilcoxon on bus factor finds no difference ($W = 5.0$, $p = 1.00$, 4 of 37 change) --- bots distort fine-grained concentration metrics (HHI) but not coarser thresholds (bus factor).

\paragraph{Week-by-week concentration (novel metric).}
We compute the elephant-week share per repository as defined in \S\ref{sec:analysis}. The per-repository median elephant-week share is \textbf{96.6\%} (IQR 94.9--100.0\%, range 43.6--100.0\%): for half the panel, at least 96.6\% of the repository's active weeks have a single dominant contributor. Pooled across all panel-weeks weighted by activity, 83.3\% of active weeks are elephant weeks; the lower pooled figure is dominated by the largest repositories, which are also the most collaborative.

\subsection{Challenge 3: Effort concentration exceeds activity concentration}\label{sec:gini}

\emph{This is a novel finding. We are not aware of prior literature reporting a paired within-repository comparison of effort-weighted vs commit-count Gini across an OSS-domain panel.}

The paired comparison of effort-Gini (lines changed per contributor) and activity-Gini (commits per contributor) within each of the 37 repositories yields a within-repository design robust to per-metric coverage gaps. Full-history line-Gini has median 0.70 (IQR 0.21). Line-Gini exceeds commit-Gini in 27 of 37 repositories, is smaller in 6, and is equal in 4; mean $\Delta = +0.052$. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 33 non-zero pairs rejects the null of zero difference: \textbf{$W = 53.0$, $p = 4.8 \times 10^{-5}$, rank-biserial $r = +0.81$} (very large effect). A one-sided sign test on 27/33 positive differences gives $p = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}$. At the largest scales ($>$~5{,}000 commits) the line-Gini saturates near 1 while the commit-Gini stays at 0.76--0.95, indicating that a small number of large-line-count commits dominate effort concentration at scale even when commit-count concentration looks moderate. Figure~\ref{fig:gini} shows the paired comparison.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{fig_effort_gini_clean.png}
\caption{Effort Gini (lines) vs.\ effort Gini (commits) per repository. Points above the $y=x$ diagonal are repositories where effort concentration exceeds activity concentration. The pattern is consistent across 27 of 37 repositories (Wilcoxon $W = 53.0$, $p = 4.8 \times 10^{-5}$, rank-biserial $r = +0.81$). Marker size encodes contributor count.}
\label{fig:gini}
\end{figure}

The substantive implication is that any sustainability analysis using commit counts as a proxy for contribution weight \emph{systematically under-estimates} effort concentration in civic-tech projects. CHAOSS-aligned health frameworks should incorporate effort-resolved metrics alongside count-based ones.

\subsection{Challenge 4: Stale-issue backlogs and missing release discipline}\label{sec:backlogs}

\paragraph{Stale issues.}
Among the 26 repositories with non-zero open issues, the median stale-issue ratio (open issues with no activity in 90 days) is \textbf{0.98} (IQR 0.26): for the median repository, $\approx$98\% of its open issues have had no activity in the last quarter. Only three repositories in the panel keep their stale ratio below 0.50. The change-request acceptance ratio has median 0.82 (IQR 0.13, $n=36$); responsiveness on pull requests is substantially better than on issues (median PR review turnaround 3.2~h ($n=29$) vs.\ median issue first-response 34.4~h ($n=24$)). The coverage of these two metrics differs (29/37 vs.\ 24/37 repositories with non-zero matching data), so the comparison is between partly different sub-panels; the magnitude of the gap ($\approx$10$\times$) is too large to be explained by coverage alone, but precise contrasts would require a coverage-matched analysis we do not attempt here.

\paragraph{Documentation.}
The GitHub community-health percentage (presence of README, CONTRIBUTING, CODE\_OF\_CONDUCT, issue/PR templates, license) has median 50\% across the panel (IQR 25\%, range 0--100\%); only three repositories achieve 100\%, and one scores 0\%. Many civic-tech projects lack the standard community documentation that supports contributor onboarding --- which compounds the drive-by problem of Challenge~1.

\paragraph{Release discipline.}
\textbf{Twenty of 37 repositories (54\%) have zero releases ever}, despite the median age of the no-release group being 3.7 years. Five projects in the no-release group are more than nine years old, including a 15-year-old freedom-of-information platform and an 11-year-old polling-station service. Among the 17 repositories that do release, the median cadence is 6.3 releases/year (IQR 1.4--32.6) --- a healthy rate by commercial-OSS standards, but characteristic of a minority of the panel. Civic-tech projects largely do not adopt semantic versioning, which complicates downstream deployment, security-patch tracking, and dependency management for organisations that consume them.

\subsection{Challenge 5: Activity over project age --- the survivor paradox}\label{sec:age}

A naive intuition would predict that civic-tech projects decay with age: founders move on, grant funding lapses, attention drifts. The panel shows the opposite within the surviving projects: \textbf{median weekly commit count rises with project age} (Figure~\ref{fig:age}). Median weekly commits move from $\approx$11 in the first year of a project's life to $\approx$30 by year 8 and $\approx$50 by year 11, before sample size collapses past year 13. Among the 27 repositories with sufficient \texttt{weekly\_snapshots} coverage, none has zero commits in the last 26 weeks of our data window.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig_activity_vs_age.png}
\caption{Weekly commit count vs.\ project age across the panel. Each dot is one repo-week. The orange line is the median per-year bucket. Median activity rises with age, but the panel selects on survival --- the projects that did not make it to age 10 are not in the data.}
\label{fig:age}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{This is survivor bias, not health.}
Our panel includes only projects with $\geq 1$ commit in the preceding 12 months (selection criterion C3-derived). Civic-tech projects that died are excluded by construction. The rising-with-age curve therefore characterises the projects that persisted, not the cohort that started at the same time. Splitting the panel at the median age (6.3 years) confirms that mature projects ($n = 19$) differ substantially from young ($n = 18$) on size proxies (Mann--Whitney $U$ on \texttt{num\_developers}: $p = 0.004$, Cliff's $\delta = +0.61$ large; on \texttt{total\_commits}: $p = 0.009$, $\delta = +0.56$ large; on \texttt{bus\_factor\_no\_bots}: $p = 0.036$, $\delta = +0.46$ medium--large), but the \texttt{stale\_issue\_ratio} comparison yields only a small effect ($\delta = +0.24$). A proper survival analysis requires either tracking a 2010s-vintage cohort prospectively from origin or assembling a comparison group of unmaintained projects (\S\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses both as planned extensions). The substantive observation that survives selection is narrower but real: \emph{the surviving civic-tech projects in the panel show no evidence of activity decline}, consistent with a model in which projects either die outright or stabilise into a long-lived activity regime.

\subsection{Challenge 6: The thin and umbrella-bounded cross-project ecosystem}\label{sec:ecosystem}

\emph{The sensitivity-aware version of this finding is novel; it sharpens the headline-rate claim by controlling for the umbrella-network composition of our sampling frame.}

The 37 repositories share 511 unique contributor logins overall, of whom 498 are human. \textbf{Only 12.3\% of contributors are active in more than one panel project} (63 of 511; 59 of 498 humans). The most cross-project bot is \texttt{dependabot[bot]} in 23 of 37 repositories; the most cross-project human, \texttt{kilemensi}, contributes to 13 repositories all under Code for Africa.

\paragraph{Sensitivity check (sampling-frame bias).}
A reviewer's concern: our sampling frame seeded heavily from umbrella networks (Code for America, Code for Africa, MySociety, Democracy Club, OKF, Code for Japan), so an ``umbrella-shaped ecosystem'' finding could be an artefact of how we sampled. We address this by re-running the cross-project analysis at the \emph{organisation} level: of the 113 human contributors active in $\geq 2$ panel repositories (computed from the lifecycle table; \S\ref{sec:data-availability}), only \textbf{11 (9.7\%) span $\geq 2$ distinct stewarding organisations}. The remaining 102 (90.3\%) of cross-project humans stay within a single organisation --- most often Code for Africa, secondarily Code for America. Translating to the all-contributor denominator: of the 2{,}056 unique humans on the panel, $\approx$5.5\% are panel-cross-project and $\approx$0.5\% are cross-organisational. The umbrella-shaped pattern therefore is not a sampling artefact: it survives at the organisation level, where umbrella affiliations cannot inflate the cross-project rate. Figure~\ref{fig:cross} shows the top cross-project humans color-coded by single-organisation vs.\ multi-organisation participation.

\paragraph{Implication.}
The civic-tech cross-project contributor pool is not just small (5.5\% of all panel humans); it is umbrella-bounded (0.5\% genuinely cross-organisational). The most-mobile contributors clustered around two ``targeted-additions'' repositories (\texttt{mastodon/mastodon}, \texttt{meshtastic/firmware}), suggesting that flagship-scale technically-distinctive projects attract the rare cross-network contributor. Sustainability interventions that aim to grow a broader cross-cutting civic-tech contributor class would need cross-umbrella programming explicitly --- the data does not support an assumption that such a class exists today.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What ``sustainable'' would actually look like.}
The challenges in \S\ref{sec:results} characterise a population: most contributors arrive once, most projects have a bus factor of 1 or 2, most open issues are stale, most projects have never tagged a release, and most contributors do not move between projects. None of these is fatal individually; together they sketch an ecosystem sustained by a small active core supplemented by drive-by patches. Several of the individual patterns (drive-by dominance, low bus factor, high stale-issue ratio) are consistent with prior findings on small-team OSS~\cite{Pinto2016,Avelino2016,Coelho2017}, so the descriptive picture is not by itself uniquely civic-tech; what is civic-tech-specific is the \emph{stakes} of failure --- when these patterns play out in a freedom-of-information platform or a voter-information service, the cost is paid by democratic participation. A direct quantitative comparison against a matched commercial-OSS panel is the natural next step (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}). A defensible operational definition of ``sustainable civic-tech project'' for a longitudinal extension would require simultaneous progress on bus factor (raise to $\geq 3$), stale ratio (lower to $< 0.5$), and active-vs-departed ratio (raise active count without merely accumulating drive-by patches).

\paragraph{Bot filtering matters --- but selectively.}
Bots significantly inflate HHI (Wilcoxon $p = 7 \times 10^{-6}$) but do not materially affect bus factor ($p = 1.00$) or elephant factor (no change in any repository). We recommend bot filtering as standard practice when computing HHI; bus-factor analyses can safely include bots. The bot landscape is dominated by \texttt{dependabot[bot]} (23 of 37 repositories) and \texttt{github-actions[bot]} (7 of 37) --- automated dependency maintenance is widespread in the panel even where human review processes are not.

\paragraph{Effort-based measurement matters.}
The systematic positive gap between line-Gini and commit-Gini (mean $\Delta = +0.052$, 27 of 37 positive, Wilcoxon $p = 4.8 \times 10^{-5}$) demonstrates that commit-count concentration under-estimates effort concentration. The implication for downstream OSS-health frameworks is that lines-changed-per-contributor should accompany count-based concentration metrics, especially in small-team contexts where individual large refactors or batch merges shape effort distribution.

\paragraph{The cross-project ecosystem is umbrella-shaped, not panel-shaped.}
The fact that the top-10 cross-project humans cluster within Code for Africa, Code for America, and Democracy Club suggests that civic-tech contributor flow is organised by umbrella network rather than by topic, technology, or geography. Sustainability interventions that aim to grow a broader cross-cutting civic-tech contributor class would need to provide cross-umbrella programming explicitly --- the data does not support an assumption that such a class exists already.

\paragraph{Survivor bias bounds what we can claim about evolution.}
The age-vs-activity pattern (Challenge 5) is informative about survivors but not about cohorts: we cannot from this panel alone make claims about how civic-tech projects evolve, only about how the ones that persisted now behave. The longitudinal extension (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}) and a planned ``failed projects'' comparison group are the right ways to convert this into causal claims about lifecycle.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
Bus factor, HHI, and the elephant factor are commit-based and undervalue contributors who primarily review code, manage issues, or write documentation; the effort-Gini analysis (\S\ref{sec:gini}) mitigates this for the lines-changed dimension. Inclusion criteria C1--C3 are applied by two coders with disagreements resolved by discussion; until $\kappa$ is reported in the camera-ready, the single-rule operationalisation of ``design intent at project inception'' remains a residual construct-validity threat. We computed Cliff's $\delta$ for the Mann--Whitney comparisons and rank-biserial $r$ for paired Wilcoxon tests; all reported effect sizes are large.

\paragraph{External validity and sampling-frame selection.}
The 37-repository panel is purposive, not a probability sample of any defined civic-tech population, and may not generalise to all civic-tech projects, particularly self-hosted or non-GitHub projects. The civic-tech definition emphasises explicit public-interest design intent rather than incidental public-interest use; broader definitions would change sample composition. The artefact deposit lists repositories by name and several are uniquely identifiable on inspection; ESEM allows this for anonymised submissions.

The sampling frame seeded heavily from umbrella networks (six umbrella organisations supply 24 of 37 panel repos), so the panel over-represents umbrella-affiliated civic-tech projects relative to independent ones. This affects Challenge 6 most directly: cross-project rates within umbrella-network repos (27.1\% of contributors) are higher than within non-umbrella repos (3.1\%), so the panel's headline 12.3\% cross-project rate is partly an artefact of which repos we sampled. The organisation-level sensitivity check in \S\ref{sec:ecosystem} (only 9.7\% of cross-project humans span $\geq 2$ stewarding organisations) addresses this concern by recomputing at the level of cross-organisational mobility, which is invariant to umbrella over-representation. We retain ``umbrella-bounded'' as the defensible characterisation; the absolute panel-cross-project rate would change under a different sampling frame.

\paragraph{Statistical validity.}
$n = 37$ limits power for detecting small effects in inferential tests; we treat the inferential layer as exploratory and emphasise the paired-design Wilcoxon results, which have large effect sizes and within-repository controls. Per-metric coverage is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:coverage}.

The longitudinal extension and a planned comparison group of unmaintained civic-tech projects (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}) are the appropriate corrections.

\section{Conclusions and Longer-Term Plan}\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented emerging results from a multi-dimensional landscape analysis of 37 civic-tech repositories. Six sustainability challenges emerge from the data: drive-by contribution dominates (52\% single-commit contributors), cores are highly concentrated (median bus factor 2; 46\% at bus factor 1; per-repository median 96.6\% elephant weeks), effort concentration systematically exceeds activity concentration (Wilcoxon $p = 4.8 \times 10^{-5}$), stale-issue backlogs are pervasive (median ratio 0.98), release discipline is largely absent (54\% of repositories have never tagged a release), and the cross-project contributor ecosystem is thin (12.3\% cross-project) and umbrella-shaped. A counter-pattern --- surviving older projects intensify rather than decay --- characterises survivors only and reflects selection bias rather than ecosystem health.

The work is in progress along four axes. \textbf{(L1) Longitudinal tracking}: quarterly recrawls of the 37-repository panel over a 24-month horizon, with change-over-time analyses on the six challenges and event-study designs around governance changes (sponsor changes, license relicensing, foundation transitions). \textbf{(L2) Replication and extension}: extension to non-GitHub hosts (GitLab, Codeberg, self-hosted Gitea) and to a larger civic-tech population (target $n \geq 100$). \textbf{(L3) Failed-projects comparison group}: a parallel panel of civic-tech repositories that have ceased activity in the last five years, supporting causal claims about which sustainability characteristics distinguish survivors from non-survivors. \textbf{(L4) Matched commercial-OSS comparison panel}: a same-size panel of small-team commercial-OSS projects (matched on language, age, team size) crawled with the same toolchain, to test whether the six civic-tech challenges differ in magnitude from comparable non-civic-tech projects --- the addressable form of the ``how does civic-tech compare to general OSS?'' question.

The toolchain, the canonical 37-repository dataset, the dual-coder agreement table, deterministic figure-regeneration scripts, and a per-repository findings document are open-source and Zenodo-archived.
